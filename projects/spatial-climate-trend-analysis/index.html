Where is Zambia warming fastest? Thirty years of climate model trends by grid cell and district

Global averages are easy to quote, but nobody farms the globe. A district officer in Zambia needs to know what is changing in their own area, how fast, and whether the change is real or just year-to-year noise.

I took 30 years of daily output from the MIROC6 climate model (CMIP6 historical run, 1981 to 2010), cut out Zambia with its 115 district boundaries, and tested every grid cell for a trend in daytime highs, night-time lows and rainfall. The whole country warmed in the simulation, the east fastest and the west slowest. Night-time warming is the most reliable signal in the data. Rainfall shows a drier north and a wetter west, but none of that pattern survives a correction for multiple testing.

Memory Dube, October 2026. Built with Python (xarray, rioxarray, geopandas, pymannkendall), CMIP6 MIROC6 and GADM district boundaries.

The question

I wanted to answer three questions for every part of Zambia. How big is the trend? Is it statistically real? And does it differ across the country? A national figure hides the answer to the third question, which is the one that matters for planning, so the analysis runs grid cell by grid cell and then rolls up to districts and provinces.

The variables are the three that drive most climate impacts on farming and health: the daily maximum temperature, the daily minimum temperature and rainfall.

MIROC6 is one of the CMIP6 global climate models developed in Japan. The historical experiment is driven by observed greenhouse gases, aerosols and solar changes, so its trends show how the model thinks Zambia’s climate responded to that forcing. It is a simulation, not a record of what weather stations measured, and I come back to that in the limitations.

The model stores temperature in kelvin and rainfall as a flux in kilograms per square metre per second. I converted temperature to °C and multiplied the rainfall flux by 86,400 seconds to get millimetres per day.

Method

  1. Clip each daily field to the district boundaries with rioxarray. Cells whose centre falls outside Zambia become empty, leaving 32 cells.
  2. Build annual series, using the mean for temperature and the total for rainfall. A year only counts if it has at least 300 valid days, and every Zambian cell passed in every year.
  3. Fit Sen’s slope to each cell’s 30 annual values and run the Mann-Kendall test at α = 0.05.
  4. Re-test with the Hamed-Rao correction for autocorrelation and apply a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction across the 32 cells.
  5. Average the cells into a national series (weighted by the cosine of latitude), an area-weighted value for each province, and a lookup from each district to the cell covering most of it.
  6. Map the trends with symmetric diverging colours so that zero is always the neutral colour.

Sen’s slope is the median of the slopes between every pair of years, so one extreme year can’t drag the trend around the way it can with ordinary least squares. The Mann-Kendall test works on ranks and makes no assumption about the data being normally distributed, which suits rainfall in particular.

The two extra checks matter because the basic test can flatter the results. One warm year tends to follow another, which makes the plain Mann-Kendall test too confident, and the Hamed-Rao version allows for that. Testing 32 cells at the 5% level also means one or two will look significant by chance, and the false discovery rate correction controls for that. A trend that survives both is one I am comfortable reporting.

Daytime highs

Every cell warmed, at rates between 0.10 and 0.86 °C per decade with a mean of 0.45. The national average of daily highs rose from 31.2 °C in the 1980s to 32.1 °C in the 2000s, a Sen slope of 0.42 °C per decade (p = 0.027).

The pattern runs east to west. The three fastest cells sit over Nyimba, Petauke and Sinda (+0.86 °C per decade), over Chipata and the surrounding Eastern districts (+0.83) and over Serenje and Chitambo in Central Province (+0.83). The slowest are in Western Province, from +0.10 °C over Kalabo to +0.13 over Mongu, and neither of those is significant. Western daytime temperatures swing so much from year to year that a trend of that size can’t be separated from the noise.

Of the 32 cells, 17 pass the basic test. That drops to 12 with the autocorrelation correction and 11 after the false discovery rate correction, all of them in the centre, east and north-east.

Night-time lows

Night-time lows warmed more slowly, between 0.19 and 0.44 °C per decade with a mean of 0.30, but far more consistently. Nationally they rose from 18.2 °C to 18.9 °C between the first and last decades (0.28 °C per decade, p = 0.005). Thirty-one of the 32 cells are significant, and all 31 stay significant after the false discovery rate correction. The one exception is the far south-west around Shang’ombo, Nalolo and Sioma, which just misses at p = 0.054.

The reason the night signal is so much clearer is variability. Minimum temperatures move far less from one year to the next than maximum temperatures do, so a steady upward drift stands out. The fastest night-time warming is again in the centre and east, peaking at +0.44 °C per decade over Serenje and Chitambo.

Rainfall

Rainfall shows a clear spatial contrast and no confirmed trend. The north and north-east dry, by up to 89 mm per decade in the cell over Nsama, Kaputa, Mporokoso, Chiengi and Nchelenge. Parts of the west get wetter, by up to 68 mm per decade over Kabompo and Manyinga. By province, Luapula, Northern, Copperbelt and Muchinga lose 53 to 70 mm per decade, while North-Western and Southern gain about 13 to 15 mm.

Only the far-northern cell passes the basic test (p = 0.038), and it does not survive the false discovery rate correction. With 32 tests at the 5% level, one false positive is roughly what chance alone would produce. The national trend of -18 mm per decade has p = 0.57. Against the model’s median annual rainfall of about 1,525 mm, the median cell trend works out at around -1.8% per decade, which is small next to the year-to-year swings. I read the north-south contrast as a hypothesis worth testing with more models, not as a result.

The gap between day and night

Because daytime highs warmed faster than night-time lows, the daily temperature range widened, from 13.0 °C in the 1980s to 13.3 °C in the 2000s. Drying soils and changes in cloud cover could both produce that, but the change is not significant nationally (p = 0.20), and none of the five cells that pass the basic test survive the false discovery rate correction. I would not build an argument on it with one model.

What it means

If observations confirm the model, the east is where heat matters first. Eastern, Lusaka, Muchinga, Southern and Central provinces all warm by more than 0.55 °C per decade in daytime highs, and Eastern is a major maize and cotton area. Heat-tolerant varieties, planting dates and livestock heat planning would make sense to prioritise there.

Warmer nights are the most dependable result. They raise crop water demand and change conditions for pests and disease, and the signal is strong enough in every province to plan around.

Rainfall is the opposite case. This run gives no statistical support for a drying or wetting trend anywhere, so it shouldn’t be used to justify water or irrigation decisions on its own. The north-east drying pattern is worth checking against a multi-model ensemble and against observed rainfall such as CHIRPS.

The pipeline itself transfers easily. Swapping in other CMIP6 models, or future scenarios instead of the historical run, only means changing the file names in the first cells.

Limitations

  • This is one model and one run. MIROC6 r1i1p1f1 carries its own internal variability, and a multi-model ensemble would show how much of the east-west pattern belongs to this model alone.
  • The grid is coarse. At about 150 km per cell, valleys and plateau edges are smoothed out, and several districts share a single cell, so district values describe the region around them.
  • Thirty years is the minimum for a trend, and rainfall is noisy enough to bury a weak one.
  • The model is wet. Its mean annual rainfall over Zambia is about 1,600 mm (1,151 to 2,238 mm by cell), higher than commonly observed. The trend patterns are still informative, but absolute amounts need bias correction before anyone uses them for planning.
  • These warming rates are higher than those usually reported from station records for the region, so they should be validated against observations or reanalysis before informing decisions.

The code

The analysis runs top to bottom in one Jupyter notebook. Later sections reuse variables from earlier ones, so it should be run in order.
