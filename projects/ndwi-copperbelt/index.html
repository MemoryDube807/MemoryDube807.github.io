Monitoring surface water and vegetation moisture in Copperbelt reservoirs, 1996 to 2025

A handful of reservoirs supply the towns of Zambia’s Copperbelt, and the last decade brought some of the driest rainy seasons on record. I wanted to know whether the dams are actually shrinking and whether the bush around them is drying out.

I took every usable Landsat image of five dams from 1996 to 2025, measured open water with McFeeters NDWI and leaf water with Gao NDWI, and tested each change against rainfall, sensor differences and chance. One dam stands out. Kafubu, which supplies Ndola, has lost about a quarter of its usual dry-season water since 2016, and rainfall does not explain the loss. The other four held steady, and the vegetation around all five is slightly greener now than in the late 1990s.

Memory Dube, October 2026. Built with Python (xarray, odc-stac, pymannkendall), Landsat Collection 2, Sentinel-2, ERA5 and ESA WorldCover.

The question

My aim was to detect and monitor changes in the water held by open water bodies and by vegetation canopies, using nothing more than free satellite spectral bands. I framed it as two hypotheses I could actually test.

The first is that the open-water area of five Copperbelt reservoirs declined between 1996 and 2025. The second is that the vegetation around them lost water over the same period, and that in drought years its leaf water drops before its greenness does.

The five dams run across the province from Fitula near Chingola to Kafubu near Ndola. Mindolo serves Kitwe, Makoma sits outside Luanshya and Kaluba is near Mufulira. Kafubu matters most to people, because it supplies Ndola’s water.

How a satellite sees water

A satellite records how much sunlight bounces back in several colours, including two infrared bands that our eyes can’t see. Water and leaves treat those bands very differently, and both indices are built on that difference.

Open water swallows almost all near-infrared (NIR) light but still reflects a little green, which is why lakes look blue-green. The McFeeters index compares the two.

NDWI (water) = (Green - NIR) / (Green + NIR)
SurfaceGreenNIRNDWI
Water0.060.02+0.50
Bush0.080.40-0.67
Bare soil0.120.20-0.25

Water comes out positive and land negative, so I count the pixels above zero to measure each dam. Dividing by the sum cancels out the overall brightness of the scene, which lets a slightly hazy image be compared with a clear one.

Leaves work the other way round. The air spaces between leaf cells scatter near-infrared strongly, while the water inside the cells absorbs short-wave infrared (SWIR). A well-watered leaf has high NIR and low SWIR, and as the plant dries the SWIR reflection rises.

NDWI (leaves) = (NIR - SWIR) / (NIR + SWIR)

A healthy leaf with NIR 0.40 and SWIR 0.15 scores 0.45. A thirsty one with NIR 0.35 and SWIR 0.30 scores 0.08. Leaves usually lose water before they lose chlorophyll and turn yellow, so in theory this index should fall before greenness (NDVI) does. That idea is often used to argue that NDWI gives an early warning before chlorosis, and I treated it as something to test.

Landsat is the only archive with 30 years of 30 m images, so it carries the analysis. Sentinel-2 only serves as a check that Landsat draws the same lake outlines, and the two agree with an overlap of 0.80 to 0.94 depending on the dam. MODIS is popular for vegetation monitoring, but one of its pixels covers about a quarter of most of these dams, so it can’t resolve them. The band numbers also change between Landsat generations, which is why the code selects bands by name.

Each year gets two composites. The end of the rains (March to May) is when dams are full and the bush is in leaf. The end of the dry season (August to October) is when storage is lowest and skies are clear, so it gives the more reliable water reading. Within a season I take the median of all cloud-free images, pixel by pixel. If one image still has thin haze, the median ignores it, whereas an average would be pulled down.

The record starts in 1996 because that is the first year with Landsat images at every dam, and 30 years is the period the WMO uses to define a region’s climate. Years with fewer than three clear images are left out. Figure 2 shows exactly which ones and why.

Two supporting datasets explain the satellite signal. ERA5 is a global weather reanalysis from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. I turned its monthly rainfall into SPI-6, the standardised precipitation index over December to May, which scores each rainy season against that place’s own history. A score below -1 is a drought, and that happens roughly one year in six. WorldCover is a 10 m land-cover map from ESA, and I used it to keep farmland out of the vegetation analysis, since harvesting and irrigation change leaf water whatever the weather does.

Method

  1. Search the Planetary Computer catalogue for Landsat scenes over each dam, year and season, and read only the bands needed.
  2. Convert to surface reflectance and mask cloud, cloud shadow and snow from each scene’s quality band.
  3. Build a median composite per season and year.
  4. Measure water inside each dam’s usual footprint, and leaf water of natural vegetation in a 300 m band around the shore.
  5. Correct for the difference between satellite generations and drop water readings that break the laws of physics.
  6. Test every series for a trend, a step change and a link with rainfall, then export the figures and an Excel workbook.

A dam’s usual footprint is the connected water body under its location point that was wet in at least 10% of years. Defining it this way stops a neighbouring pond or tailings dam from being counted.

Three checks did more for the result than anything else. The newer Landsat 8 and 9 sensors read leaf water about 0.028 higher than Landsat 5 and 7 over the same vegetation. Left alone, the switch to the new satellites in 2013 would look like the whole province suddenly got wetter. I estimated that offset in the years both generations were flying, allowing for the date of each image, and removed it before fitting any trend.

The second check comes from physics. Almost no rain falls between June and October, so a dam can’t hold more water at the end of the dry season than it did at the end of the rains. When the images said it did, the rainy-season reading was wrong, usually because of haze or muddy water, and I dropped it. That removed 19 of 104 readings.

The third is the statistics. I used the Mann-Kendall trend test with the Hamed-Rao correction, because one wet year tends to follow another and the standard test overstates significance when that happens. The slope is a Sen slope with a 95% interval, and a change only counts if it is significant and larger than 5% per decade. Pixel maps go through a false-discovery-rate correction, since among 25,000 pixels about 5% would look significant by chance alone.

What happened to the water

Four of the five dams have flat trend lines. Kafubu is the exception, losing 9.5 percentage points of its footprint per decade (p = 0.028 over 26 years), and it is the only dam whose decline is both significant and larger than 5% per decade. The next section goes through each dam in turn.

Kafubu Dam, Ndola

Kafubu held about 175 ha of water in the dry season from 1996 to 2005, and 177 ha from 2006 to 2015. Between 2016 and 2025 the figure dropped to 131 ha. The Pettitt test places the break around 2020, and 2024 was the lowest year in the record at 37% of the usual footprint. Most of the loss is along the shallow edges and the narrow northern arm, and the average strip of exposed shore widened from 26 m to 63 m between the second and third decades.

Drought is the obvious suspect, but the numbers point elsewhere. Year to year, Kafubu’s water barely follows rainy-season rain (Spearman ρ = 0.11, p = 0.58). The 2023 rains were well above normal, and the dam still only reached 59%. Once rainfall is taken out, the decline remains significant (p = 0.033), and a second water index, MNDWI, shows the same fall. My working explanation is a local pressure, most likely more water being drawn for Ndola or silt filling the shallow end, with the 2024 drought pushing an already stressed reservoir to its lowest point. The utility’s abstraction and level records would settle which.

The land around Kafubu is the most farmed of the five, with 37% cropland and 35% tree cover within 1.5 km. Its natural vegetation is in good shape. Among those pixels, 27% became significantly greener and fewer than 1% browner, and the worst stress year was 2002, when 68% was in drought condition, against 22% in 2025. Reliability is low because the old and new Landsat sensors disagree on the trend direction, so I treat the decline as strong evidence that still needs checking on the ground.

Mindolo Dam, Kitwe

Mindolo is the steadiest of the five. Its dry-season cover averaged 98%, 96% and 95% across the three decades. The trend test does pick up a small decline of 0.9 points per decade (p < 0.001), but that is far below the 5% per decade I treat as meaningful, and the exposed shoreline only widened from 5 m to 10 m. In 2024 its water was slightly above normal.

The land around it is mostly grassland (40%), with 28% tree cover, 20% shrubland and almost no farming. It shows the strongest greening of the five dams, at 0.060 NDVI per decade, and its leaf water is rising too (p = 0.014). The rainy season of 2024 still left a mark, with 38% of its natural vegetation in drought condition, the highest share at any dam since 2015. Reliability is medium, because Landsat and Sentinel-2 agree only moderately on its year-to-year changes (r = 0.53).

Makoma Dam, Luanshya

Makoma is the most changeable. Dry-season cover averaged 59% in the first decade and fell to 53% in the second, with several years in the 30s and 40s between 2014 and 2019. The dam then refilled in 2020 and 2021 and has stayed close to full since, so the third decade averages 72%. It was well above normal through the 2024 drought, at 1.7 standard deviations over its own average.

That swing leaves the 30-year trend inconclusive. The estimate is +12% per decade, but the 95% interval runs from -10% to +33% (p = 0.58). A drop and recovery this sharp, unrelated to rainfall, looks more like a change in how the dam was operated than a climate signal, and it would be worth asking its managers. Shrubland (40%) and grassland (27%) cover most of the surrounding land, with 7% cropland. Leaf water is rising clearly (p < 0.001), and natural vegetation is greener by 0.028 NDVI per decade. Four of its 19 end-of-rains readings failed the physics check, and reliability is low because the two Landsat generations disagree on the trend direction.

Fitula Dam, Chingola

Fitula is the smallest dam, with a usual footprint of only 43 ha, so each 30 m pixel is a larger share of the lake and the readings are noisier. Dry-season cover held at 87%, 89% and 91% by decade with no trend (p = 0.24). The end-of-rains series is mostly unusable, because 9 of its 12 readings failed the physics check, which usually points to muddy or weed-covered water. No image of Fitula passed the quality rules in either season of 2024, so its drought-year picture has a gap.

Fitula sits in woodland, with 48% tree cover, 22% grassland and 8% cropland. Natural vegetation is greener by 0.025 NDVI per decade, while the small area of cropland is slightly browner. The worst stress year was 2005, with 66% of the vegetation in drought condition. Reliability is low, mainly because Landsat and Sentinel-2 don’t agree on its year-to-year changes, which is what I would expect for a lake this small.

Kaluba Dam, Mufulira

Kaluba is the largest dam in the study at 286 ha, and one of the most stable. Dry-season cover rose slightly from 91% to 93% to 95%. That works out at 1.2% per decade, which is statistically detectable (p = 0.047) but too small to call an increase, and the exposed shoreline narrowed from 19 m to 11 m. It was above its normal level in all four drought years, including 2024 (+0.7 SD).

This is the most wooded site, with 59% tree cover and only 2% cropland. Natural vegetation greened by 0.024 NDVI per decade. The worst stress year was 2005, when 82% of the vegetation was in drought condition, compared with 13% in 2025. Reliability is medium, because Landsat and Sentinel-2 agree only moderately on its year-to-year changes (r = 0.58).

Was there drought?

Four rainy seasons since 2018 fell below -1 on the drought index: 2018 (-1.2), 2019 (-1.7), 2024 (-2.7) and 2025 (-1.7). 2024 was the driest in the record. The region received about 62% of its 1991 to 2020 average, between 55% and 69% depending on the dam, and the government declared a national disaster on 29 February 2024.

Over the full 30 years, though, wet-season rainfall has no significant trend (about -33 mm per decade, p = 0.21). The Copperbelt had a run of bad seasons rather than a steady drying. The dams also responded very differently. Mindolo, Makoma and Kaluba were at or above their normal level in 2024, while Kafubu was 2.1 standard deviations below its own average that year and 1.6 below in 2025.

Is the vegetation drying out?

What grows around a dam shapes how its vegetation signal should be read. Kaluba (59% tree cover) and Fitula (48%) sit in woodland. Mindolo is mostly grassland at 40%, and Makoma mostly shrubland at 40%. Kafubu is the farmed one, with 37% cropland within 1.5 km of the water and 35% tree cover.

Natural vegetation is getting greener at all five dams, from 0.024 NDVI per decade at Kaluba to 0.060 at Mindolo, and leaf water is flat or rising. Around Kafubu, 27% of natural-vegetation pixels became significantly greener after the false-discovery correction and fewer than 1% became browner. Cropland behaves differently from dam to dam. It is greener at Mindolo and browner at Fitula and Kaluba, which suggests farm management rather than climate.

Stress turns up in particular years. The worst were 2002 and 2005, when 66% to 87% of the natural vegetation around the dams was in drought condition. Since 2015 no dam has gone above 38%, and in 2024 the share ranged from 17% to 38%. Part of the reason 2024 looks mild is timing. Its dry spell came in January and February, and by the March to May images much of the canopy had recovered.

Early warning

To test whether leaf water warns before leaves yellow, I looked month by month at the drought years and recorded which index first fell below -0.5 standard deviations. Out of 47 dam-seasons, NDWI went first in 8 and NDVI went first in 5. In 14 they dropped in the same month, and 20 showed no stress at all. With yearly composites the lead vanishes completely.

There is a hint of an early signal, but it is too weak to rely on. Monthly Landsat composites are simply too coarse in time for this question, and Sentinel-2, which revisits every five days, would be the right data to test it properly.

What it means

For Ndola, the result that matters is Kafubu. The reservoir has lost about a quarter of its dry-season water and rainfall doesn’t account for it. The utility’s abstraction and water-level records would show whether rising demand, silt or both are responsible. A seasonal satellite check costs nothing to run, and an alert set at around 60% dry-season cover would have flagged the problem several years before 2024.

The other four reservoirs came through all four drought seasons without a lasting loss, so this data gives no reason for a province-wide response. Attention and money should go to Kafubu first.

Four failed rainy seasons in eight years is reason enough to keep drought plans ready to switch on. What the data does not show is a region that is permanently drying, so the planning problem is recurring shocks.

Around Kafubu, farms reach close to the water. A strip of natural vegetation along the shore is a cheap way to reduce the soil washing into the reservoir, and it would be worth measuring how much of the loss is silt.

Anyone building a drought alert from this kind of data should pair leaf-water NDWI with a rainfall index instead of relying on NDWI alone.

Limitations

  • A 30 m pixel blurs shorelines. Moving the water threshold from -0.1 to +0.1 changes the areas by 8% to 146%, so I treat hectares as approximate and rely on the direction of change.
  • Three of the five dams, Kafubu included, are rated low reliability. At those sites the old and new Landsat generations disagree on the trend direction, or Landsat and Sentinel-2 disagree from year to year.
  • End-of-rains images are often cloudy or show muddy water, which is why 19 of 104 readings failed the physics check.
  • The land-cover map is from 2021 and is applied to all 30 years, so earlier clearing for farms is not captured.
  • There is no ground truth yet. The results should be checked against reservoir level records.
