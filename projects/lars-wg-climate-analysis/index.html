How much hotter and drier could Mount Makulu get by 2050? Five climate models run through a weather generator

Global climate models work on grid boxes 100 to 250 km across, but a farmer or a crop breeder needs the weather at one place, day by day. Mount Makulu, Zambia’s central agricultural research station at Chilanga just south of Lusaka, has 30 years of daily records, which makes it a good place to bring the models down to a single site.

I used the station’s 1981 to 2010 record to calibrate the LARS-WG 8 weather generator and then to validate it, checking that its synthetic weather matches what was actually measured. With the generator trusted, I fed it the climate changes projected by five CMIP6 models under two emissions scenarios (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5) and generated 100 years of daily weather for two future periods, 2021 to 2040 and 2031 to 2050. Every result on this page is a future projection set against that observed 1981 to 2010 baseline.

Every run is warmer. Days of 35 °C or more, about 8 a year in the baseline, become 19 to 29 a year in the middle estimate, most of them between September and November. Most runs are drier, and the loss falls mainly in November to January, the start of the growing season. The models disagree on how much, and that disagreement is larger than the gap between the two scenarios.

The question

Climate projections usually arrive as maps of grid boxes and averages over decades. Planning at a research station needs something closer to the ground: daily weather at one site, in the seasons that matter for crops. I wanted to know how temperature and rainfall at Mount Makulu are likely to change over the next 25 years compared with 1981 to 2010, which seasons carry the change, and how much the answer depends on the choice of climate model and emissions scenario.

The year is split the way Zambia’s rainfall splits it. November to January is the core of the rains and the planting window. February to April is the end of the rains. May to July is cool and dry, and August to October is the hot, dry build-up before the next season.

The station record is fairly complete. Rainfall is missing for seven dry-season months between 2005 and 2008, and temperature has gaps in 11 of the 30 years, mostly in the mid-1990s and mid-2000s. Monthly rainfall totals only count when the month is complete, and the baseline counts of hot days and warm nights only use the 19 years with full temperature records.

Method

  1. Calibration: LARS-WG reads the observed 1981 to 2010 series and learns the station’s climate month by month: the lengths of wet and dry spells, how much falls on a wet day, and the distributions of daily minimum and maximum temperature.
  2. Validation: the calibrated generator produces a synthetic 1981 to 2010, which is tested against the observations for means, distributions, spell lengths and variability.
  3. Projection: for each model, scenario and future period, LARS-WG shifts the calibrated statistics by that model’s projected monthly changes in rainfall and temperature and generates 100 years of daily weather.
  4. Comparison: in R, each of the 20 runs is summarised into monthly and seasonal climatologies and compared with the observed 1981 to 2010 baseline. The five models are kept separate, then summarised by their median and range.
  5. Extremes: hot days (35 °C or more), warm nights (20 °C or more) and heavy rain days (30 mm or more) are counted for the baseline and for every run, along with the spread of October to April rainfall totals.

The 100 synthetic years are not a forecast of 2031, 2032 and so on. They are 100 samples of the weather the projected climate could produce, which gives steadier averages and better counts of rare days than a single 20-year model run would.

Counts of extremes are made model by model. Averaging five independent synthetic days into one gives sensible monthly means but smooths away the hot days and heavy storms.

Calibration and validation

A projection from a weather generator is only as good as its copy of today’s climate, so this step comes before any future numbers. After calibrating on 1981 to 2010, LARS-WG generated a synthetic version of the same period and compared it with the station record using Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests on daily distributions and spell lengths, t-tests on monthly means and F-tests on the year-to-year variance of monthly means.

The averages hold up. All 12 monthly means pass for rainfall, maximum and minimum temperature, and so do the daily distributions in every month with rain. Mean annual rainfall comes out at 840 mm against 838 mm observed, and mean temperatures are within 0.1 °C. Seven of the eight spell tests pass. The one failure is the length of dry spells in June to August, a season in which it almost never rains at Mount Makulu, so it has no practical effect.

Variability is where the generator falls short. For temperature, all 24 variance tests fail: the year-to-year standard deviation of a month’s mean is 0.2 to 0.6 °C in the synthetic series against 0.5 to 1.4 °C observed. Rainfall variance passes in 9 of 10 months, but whole seasons are still too uniform, because the generator draws each month largely on its own and misses the runs of wet or dry months that El Niño and La Niña bring. The future runs, which share these calibrated parameters, spread their October to April totals over 164 mm (the middle half of seasons) against 321 mm in the observations.

The heaviest daily rainfall is reproduced in the core months, December and January, and somewhat overstated in February to April and October. By season the synthetic baseline is about 13 mm wetter than observed in November to January and 16 mm drier in February to April. That error is small next to the projected November to January changes, but similar in size to the February to April ones, so I don’t lean on those.

The conclusion I draw is that the projections can be trusted for shifts in average conditions and in the frequency of hot days. They understate how often a whole season will turn out very dry or very hot.

Temperature

All 20 runs are warmer than the 1981 to 2010 baseline over the year. Projected daily highs rise by a median of 1.4 °C in 2021 to 2040 and 1.8 °C in 2031 to 2050 under SSP2-4.5, and by 1.5 and 2.1 °C under SSP5-8.5. Nights warm a little less, by 1.0 to 1.8 °C in the median.

The models spread widely. MIROC6 is the hottest in every case, 2.9 to 3.5 °C above the baseline in daytime, and MPI-ESM1-2-LR the coolest at 0.4 to 0.7 °C. The other three sit between 1.3 and 2.3 °C.

Daytime warming is strongest in November to January, a median of 1.6 to 2.5 °C depending on the run. That is also the season that loses the most rain, and the two go together: less rain means less cloud and drier soils, so more of the sun’s energy heats the air instead of evaporating water.

Hot days show the change most plainly. In 1981 to 2010 the station averaged 7.6 days a year at 35 °C or more, nearly all in September to November. The median projection is 19 a year in 2021 to 2040 and 27 to 29 in 2031 to 2050, still in the same three months. Warm nights of 20 °C or more go from 14 a year to between 46 and 75.

Rainfall

In 1981 to 2010 the station averaged 841 mm a year, 62% of it in November to January. Most projections are drier. The median change in annual rainfall is a loss of 125 mm (15%) in 2021 to 2040 and 122 mm in 2031 to 2050 under SSP2-4.5, where all five models are drier. Under SSP5-8.5 the median loss is about 56 mm (7%) and three of the five models are drier. CanESM5 is the clear exception, 98 mm wetter in 2021 to 2040 under SSP5-8.5.

Nearly all of the change falls in November to January, with a median loss of 36 to 95 mm. MRI-ESM2-0 is the driest model, removing about 150 to 160 mm (30%) from that season in every run. Projected February to April changes are small, between a loss of 19 mm and a gain of 6 mm in the median, and May to October stays as dry as it was.

Figure 4 shows where in the calendar the loss sits. It is spread over November to February, and in proportion it is largest at the start of the season: the median November is 13 to 36% drier than in 1981 to 2010, against 9 to 16% for January. The rains build more slowly. March barely changes, and April gains about 10 mm, though April is also a month where the validation found the generator about 13 mm too wet.

Heavy rain days of 30 mm or more barely move, from 7.2 a year to between 6 and 7. The middle October to April season total drops from 774 mm in the baseline to between 721 and 793 mm.

Models and scenarios

Over this period the choice of model matters far more than the choice of scenario. For annual rainfall the five models span 150 to 280 mm within each scenario, while the two scenario medians differ by about 65 mm. For daytime temperature MIROC6 and MPI-ESM1-2-LR are 2.4 to 2.8 °C apart in every run, while SSP5-8.5 adds at most 0.3 °C to the SSP2-4.5 median.

The rainfall medians even run the “wrong” way, with SSP2-4.5 drier than SSP5-8.5. That is not a sign that higher emissions bring more rain. Before 2050 the two scenarios have barely separated, and each 20-year window carries its model’s own natural ups and downs, which can easily outweigh the scenario signal. The two periods also share the years 2031 to 2040, so they aren’t independent.

In practice that means planning with the full range of models and testing decisions against the driest model (MRI-ESM2-0) and the hottest (MIROC6) as well as the median.

What it means

The change lands at a sensitive time of year. The extra hot days pile up in September to November, when soils are driest and the first rains are due, and November to January is both the planting window and the season that loses most rain. A hotter, drier start puts more pressure on germination and early crop establishment, and strengthens the case for early-maturing and heat-tolerant varieties, conservation farming that holds soil moisture, and planting decisions tied to the actual onset of the rains.

Warmer nights matter too. They raise crop water demand, speed up crop development and change conditions for pests and disease, and they rise in every run.

Because the generator understates year-to-year variability, these numbers describe the average future season. The worst seasons in that future will be hotter and drier than the averages suggest.

Limitations

  • The validation is in-sample: the generator was tested on the same 30 years it was calibrated on. A split-sample check, calibrating on 1981 to 1995 and testing on 1996 to 2010, would be a stronger test and is the next step.
  • This is one station. Mount Makulu represents the Lusaka plateau; valley sites and the wetter north will differ.
  • LARS-WG 8 ships 15 CMIP6 models and this analysis uses five. A larger set would show the full range better, especially for rainfall, where these five disagree on sign.
  • The projections are compared with the observed baseline because the synthetic baseline series wasn’t saved. The validation puts the difference between the two at about 15 mm per season and under 0.1 °C.
  • The generator understates year-to-year variability, so very dry or very hot seasons will be more frequent than these runs suggest.
  • The projections stop at 2050, before the scenarios pull far apart.

The code
