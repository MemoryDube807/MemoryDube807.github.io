FloodSense Zambia: a 48-hour flood check tested against 18 real floods

Pick any point in Zambia and this tool pulls the live rainfall forecast, the last month of rain, the flow of the nearest river and the shape of the land around it, then says how likely flooding is over the next two days and what to do about it.

The first version of this calculator asked the user to type in rainfall, elevation, drainage and soil, and scored them with weights I had picked by hand. When I tested it, two problems stood out. It scored height above sea level, so every Zambian town got the minimum elevation score. And its HIGH level was almost impossible to reach in Zambia: across 18 documented floods it would have issued HIGH for 3 of them, and only if the user said the drainage was poor and the soil clay. I rebuilt it around live data and measured each factor against the place’s own climate, then backtested the new score on 29 rainy seasons at 12 places that flooded between 2017 and 2026.

Memory Dube, October 2026. Built with Python (pandas, Flask, pytest), JavaScript, Open-Meteo, ERA5, GloFAS and the Copernicus DEM.

What was wrong with the old calculator

The first version took four inputs, rainfall over 48 hours, elevation, drainage and soil type, turned each into points from a lookup table and added them with weights of 40, 30, 20 and 10. It looked reasonable and ran nicely, but three things stopped it working in the real world.

Elevation was height above sea level, with anything under 5 m scoring highest. That logic belongs to coastal flooding. All of Zambia lies above about 330 m, so every Zambian location got the lowest elevation score, and the factor with 30% of the weight did nothing. What matters inland is how low a spot sits compared with the ground and streams around it.

Rainfall was judged against fixed millimetre thresholds, so 60 mm counted the same in dry Lundazi as in wet Samfya, and the same on parched ground in November as on soaked ground in February.

Nothing had been tested. Once elevation is stuck at its minimum, reaching HIGH needs at least 100 mm in 48 hours plus poor drainage and clay soil. Run against the 18 floods in this study, that rule issued HIGH for 3. With moderate drainage and loam it never issued HIGH at all.

Flood records in Zambia are thin. Reports often give a week rather than a day and a district rather than a village, so each flood has a date window and a point at the district centre or named town. I used windows as reported and let the score peak one day before the first reported date, since rain usually comes before the water.

How the score works

The score asks three questions, each measured against the place’s own climate.

  1. Rain: how does the 48-hour forecast compare with the largest 2-day fall of a typical year at this place? That reference is the median of the yearly maximum 2-day rainfall from 1991 to 2019, roughly a once-in-two-years fall.
  2. Ground wetness: how much rain fell over the previous 30 days, weighting recent days more (each day back counts 90% of the one after)? It is compared with the wettest 10% of rainy-season days at the same place.
  3. River: how does the forecast flow at the nearest GloFAS river cell compare with its usual yearly peak? Where the yearly peak is under 1 m³/s there is no real river at the point, and this share follows ground wetness instead.

Each ratio is capped, at 1.5 for rain and wetness and 2 for the river, so one extreme factor can’t carry the whole score. They are weighted 50, 30 and 20 and scaled to 100. A score of 35 or more is MODERATE and 50 or more is HIGH.

I set the weights before running the backtest, on the reasoning that rain is the trigger and the other two set the stage. Afterwards I tried ten other combinations, from 40/20/40 to 70/30/0. The average rank of the flood windows barely moved (0.830 to 0.848 against 0.844 for my choice), so with 18 floods there is no basis for fine-tuning and I kept the simpler, reasoned version.

Exposure is applied separately. The live tool reads terrain heights on a grid about 2 km across and works out how far the point sits above the lowest ground nearby; a point within 5 m of it is treated as low-lying. That and the user’s drainage answer multiply the score by between 0.77 and 1.32. This part can’t be tested on the 18 floods, because each place has a single terrain value, so the page shows hazard and exposure side by side.

Backtest results

Fifteen of the 18 floods reached MODERATE or higher within their reported window, and 9 reached HIGH. Outside Lusaka the score caught all 13. The clearest signals came from rural floods driven by big rainfall: Lundazi in December 2025 (107 mm in two days, 2.5 times its 2-year fall, score 93), Lumezi and Mambwe in January 2020 (score 84 and 80) and Chongwe in January 2023, where heavy rain, wet ground and a river at nearly twice its usual peak all lined up (score 84).

The Southern Province floods of February 2023 at Monze, Mazabuka and Namwala are the interesting case. Rain on the day was modest, 20 to 29 mm, which the old rule rated LOW unless the user said the drainage was poor. What lifted the score to 43 to 52 was ground that had already been soaked for weeks. Those floods came from the Magoye and Kafue river systems filling up, and the ground-wetness factor is what picked them up.

For false alarms, the score went to MODERATE about 5 times a season and to HIGH about twice a season at each place on days with no recorded flood. Some of those will be real floods that never made the news, so the true false-alarm rate is lower than this, but I can’t say by how much. Compared at the same number of floods caught, the old rule with poor drainage and clay soil raised more than twice as many alerts.

At very strict thresholds the advantage disappears: a plain rainfall rule set at 50 mm catches 8 floods with about one alert a season, against 5 for the new score at a similar alert rate. The new score earns its place at the watch level, where it catches slow, soaked-ground floods that a rainfall threshold misses.

The Lusaka problem

Lusaka had 5 of the 18 floods and the score caught 2, both at MODERATE. For February 2025, April 2025 and January 2026 the reanalysis shows 7 to 16 mm over two days, nowhere near enough to flood anything, yet roads and homes went under.

Two things explain it. City storms are small and short, and a rainfall grid of 9 to 30 km smears an intense 40 mm downpour over Kanyama into a modest average. And in Lusaka the trigger is often the drains rather than the rain: built-over streams, blocked culverts and low-lying informal settlements flood from rainfall that would run off elsewhere. The drainage input and the low-lying terrain adjustment are there for exactly this, but they can only scale a hazard the rainfall data can see. Getting Lusaka right needs city rain gauges or radar and a drainage map, and I would treat any LOW result for Lusaka’s low-lying townships with caution during heavy storms.

Using it

The same scoring code runs in four places. The page above runs it in the browser with live data, which is why it works on GitHub Pages without a server. The Python package runs it from the command line and behind a Flask API, so it could feed an SMS or USSD service for people without smartphones, and the backtest script runs it over 29 years of history.

python -m floodsense --place Mazabuka --drainage poor
python app.py        # then GET /api/assess?lat=-15.86&lon=27.75&drainage=poor
python scripts/backtest.py

Limitations

  • Eighteen floods is a small sample. The results show the score behaves sensibly; they can’t pin the thresholds down precisely.
  • The backtest uses reanalysis rainfall, which describes what fell. The live tool uses a forecast, which adds forecast error on top.
  • Flood dates and places come from reports that are often vague, and floods that were never reported count as false alarms.
  • GloFAS models large rivers on a 5 km grid. At several places (Mazabuka, Namwala, Lundazi) the cell at the town has no real river, so the river factor falls back to ground wetness, and the real flooding river may be a few kilometres away.
  • The exposure adjustment is judgement-based and untested.
  • The tool doesn’t model dam releases, such as Kariba or Itezhi-Tezhi spilling into the Zambezi and Kafue, or dam failures like Kandesha in 2020.
